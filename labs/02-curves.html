Curves from data

In Session 4 you fitted a straight line to many points. In Session 5 you drew a curve through two. Real data often curves, and a competition gives you more than two points. This lab teaches one trick that turns the two most common curves into straight lines, so that Session 4's least squares can fit them to all the data. Then it shows why the curve that fits best is not always the one to believe. About 45 minutes in a team, with one laptop. The Senior Team has more at the end.

You need a Google Sheet. You may search, and use AI for its named jobs, with your AI Log: you will look up one record. Open each "My figures" box only when your sheet has its own answer.

1The trick

Logarithms turn multiplying into adding: ln(p × q) = ln p + ln q, and ln(xb) = b × ln x. So two curves become straight lines:

If the data followsthen this is a straight lineIts slope givesIts intercept gives
a power law, y = a × xbln y against ln x: ln y = ln a + b × ln xba = eintercept
exponential growth, y = A × gxln y against x: ln y = ln A + x × ln gg = eslopeA = eintercept

So: make a column of logarithms, fit a straight line to it with SLOPE and INTERCEPT as in Session 4, and turn the answer back with EXP. That is all.

New to logarithms? You meet them properly in Form 4, but two facts are all this lab needs, and a sheet will show you both. In any cell, compare =LN(6) with =LN(2)+LN(3), and =LN(8) with =3*LN(2). Then =EXP(LN(5)): EXP undoes LN.

8. g ≈ 1.65, so about 65% more electric vehicles every year, from a fitted start of about 212 in 2010.

9. The model says about 208,000 in October 2023 and 889,000 in September 2026. The real figures were 70,701 and about 186,000: three times and nearly five times too many.

10. Below the line from 2010 to 2013, above it from 2015 to 2017, and below again by 2019 and 2020. The early years grew slowly, the middle years fast, and since 2017 the growth has been slowing: the year-on-year factors fall from 2.7 in 2014 and 2015 to 1.04 in 2018. A constant factor cannot follow that. Session 5's growth with a ceiling bends in exactly this way.

11. The line through the logarithms, with R² ≈ 0.95, against 0.91 for the line through the counts. Yet the exponential model failed the test by a factor of three. R² measures how well a model fits the data it was given, not how well it predicts. And 0.95 is measured on the logarithms: measured on the counts themselves, the exponential model's R² is only about 0.52, because it overshoots 2019 and 2020. Quote R² only with what it was measured on, and never as the only evidence.

The method in one box

Choose the shape for a reason, before fitting: a share that cannot pass 100% needs a ceiling (Session 5); something that grows by a percentage each year is exponential; something that scales with size, such as time against distance, may be a power law.

Straighten and fit. Power law: fit ln y against ln x; b = SLOPE, a = EXP(INTERCEPT). Exponential: fit ln y against x; g = EXP(SLOPE), A = EXP(INTERCEPT). In Google Sheets, =LOGEST(y,x) gives g and A at once, and =GROWTH(y,x,new_x) predicts.

Check three things. The residuals have no pattern. A test on data the model has not seen. And the prediction stays near the range of the data, or the paper says that it does not.

4Use it

  • The Hall Problem. If your team counted a crowd at a door every ten seconds (the extra task before Session 5), plot the running total against time. A crowd that starts slowly curves at first. Does a straight line or a curve fit better, and over which seconds would you use each?
  • In a competition. Many problems give a few years of data that grow fast. Fitting them with an exponential and predicting far ahead is the mistake task 9 shows. Say in the paper how far ahead you trust the curve, and why.

My figures: open when you have tried all four

Speed is d ÷ t = d1 − b ÷ a, and doubling d multiplies it by 21 − b. For b = 1.12 that is 0.920, a fall of 8%; for b = 1.06, 0.959, a fall of about 4%.

ln(real) − ln(model) = ln(real ÷ model), and if real ÷ model = 1 + x, this is about x. A straight-line fit of t on d minimises gaps in seconds, so a 5-second miss in the 100 m costs it no more than a 5-second miss in the 10,000 m: it gives up the 100 m to fit the long races.

Riegel uses 1.06, for races from about 3.5 minutes to about 4 hours; check the original before you quote it.

Hold some matches back, fit the constant to the rest, and see how well it predicts the matches it has not seen.

import numpy as np

# Men's world records: distance in metres, time in seconds
d = np.array([100, 200, 400, 800, 1500, 5000, 10000])
t = np.array([9.58, 19.19, 43.03, 100.91, 206.00, 755.36, 1571.00])

# Straighten: ln t = ln a + b ln d, so fit a line to the logarithms
b, c = np.polyfit(np.log(d), np.log(t), 1)
a = np.exp(c)
print("t = %.4f x d^%.3f" % (a, b))
for di, ti in zip(d, t):
    model = a * di ** b
    print("%6d m  real %8.2f  model %8.2f  error %+5.1f%%" % (di, ti, model, (model - ti) / ti * 100))
m = a * 42195 ** b
print("marathon: %.0f s, about %d h %d min" % (m, m // 3600, round(m % 3600 / 60)))

# Electric vehicles in Hong Kong at the end of each year, 2010 to 2020
x = np.arange(0, 11)                       # years after 2010
n = np.array([200, 300, 500, 600, 1600, 4300, 7300, 11200, 11600, 13900, 18500])
slope, c = np.polyfit(x, np.log(n), 1)     # ln n = ln A + x ln g
g, A = np.exp(slope), np.exp(c)
print("n = %.0f x %.3f^x" % (A, g))
for label, when, real in [("October 2023", 13 + 10 / 12, 70701), ("September 2026", 16.75, 186000)]:
    print("%s: model %.0f, real %d" % (label, A * g ** when, real))

6Contest card

Fitting a curve in a competition

Use it when the data curves and you must continue it, or when you need a number, such as a rate or an exponent, from many points at once.

In the sheet: LN, SLOPE, INTERCEPT, EXP; or LOGEST and GROWTH; or a chart trend line with its equation.

What a judge wants to see: why this shape, in one sentence about the real world; the fitted equation with its units; a residual plot or a sentence about the residuals; a test on held-back data; and the range of the data, with a warning if the prediction leaves it.

What a judge distrusts: R² as the only evidence; a curve chosen because it fitted best; a prediction far outside the data with no warning; and a fitted constant never tested on data it was not fitted to.
