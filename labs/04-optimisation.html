The best of many plans

Comparing two plans tells you which is better. Many competition tasks ask for the best plan of all: the cheapest, the fastest, the fairest. Finding it is called optimisation. The Hall Problem page gives you two rules for one kind of decision. This lab gives you two methods that work for almost any: try every plan, when there are few enough, and swap, when there are too many. Then it shows the trap in swapping, and the idea behind the "simulated annealing" in the football paper. About 45 minutes in a team, with one laptop. The Senior Team has more at the end.

You need a Google Sheet, and paper for section 2. AI may help with a formula, with your AI Log. Open each "My figures" box only when your team has its own answer.

1Try every plan: the coaches

Problem

The whole school, 900 students and 30 teachers, goes to the stadium for Sports Day. A coach company offers:

  • large coaches: 61 seats, $3,100 each for the day. It has only 12.
  • medium coaches: 49 seats, $2,600 each, as many as the school needs.

Everyone needs a seat. What is the cheapest plan?

The prices are made up for this lab.

Team time: about 20 minutes.

  1. Modeller Write the model in three lines: what you choose (call them L and M), what "best" means as a formula, and every rule a plan must obey. Do not forget the rules nobody says out loud.
  2. Team A large coach costs less per seat than a medium one. So hire all 12 large coaches and fill the rest with medium ones. What does that cost, and how many seats are empty?
  3. Data lead Now try every plan. Put L = 0 to 12 down column A, from A2, and M = 0 to 20 along row 1, from B1. Put the price of a large coach, 3100, in X2, in its own cell as in Session 6. In B2 put a formula that gives the cost if the plan has enough seats, and leaves the cell empty if it does not: =IF(61*$A2+49*B$1>=930,$X$2*$A2+2600*B$1,""). Fill it across and down. The $ signs keep the formula reading column A, row 1 and X2. Find the smallest cost with =MIN(B2:V14). To colour it, select B2:V14, choose Format, then Conditional formatting, then "Custom formula is", and type =B2=MIN($B$2:$V$14). Which plan is cheapest?
  4. Every team Why does the cheapest plan not use all twelve of the cheaper-per-seat coaches?
  5. Every team The price of a large coach is only a quote. Raise X2 in steps of $10. By how much can it rise before a different plan becomes the cheapest, and which plan is that?
  6. Every team The stadium's car park holds at most 16 coaches. Add this rule to your formula: inside the IF, AND(61*$A2+49*B$1>=930,$A2+B$1<=16) is true only when both rules are kept. What happens? What would your team tell the school?
My figures: open when your team has tried all six

1. Choose whole numbers L and M. Make 3100L + 2600M as small as possible. Rules: 61L + 49M ≥ 930; L is at most 12; and L and M are whole numbers, at least 0. Nobody hires 4.04 coaches.

2. 12 large coaches seat 732, which leaves 198, so 5 medium: $37,200 + $13,000 = $50,200, with 47 empty seats.

3. 9 large and 8 medium: $48,700, with 941 seats and 11 empty. It saves $1,500 on the plan in task 2.

4. Empty seats cost money too. A price per seat counts only the seats you fill. The plan in task 2 pays for 47 seats nobody sits in.

5. By only $20. At $3,120 the plan of 4 large and 14 medium ties with 9 and 8, and above it, 4 and 14 is cheapest: 930 seats exactly, none empty. At $3,100 it costs $48,800, only $100 more than the best. A paper should give both plans, and say that the choice between them turns on the price.

6. No plan is left. Sixteen coaches hold at most 12 × 61 + 4 × 49 = 928 seats: two short. The model has done its job: it shows that the rules cannot all be kept, and the school must relax one. Two teachers could drive, a coach could make two trips, or a smaller coach could be found. A paper says which rule it relaxes, and why.

2Swap: the stalls

Problem

Four students set up four stalls for the school fair. Each sets up one stall, and each is quicker at some stalls than others, because of what he has done before. The table gives the minutes each would take.

GamesFoodBooksPlants
Adam7241130
Ben652829
Carl2462628
Dan916176

Which student should set up which stall, to make the total minutes of work as small as possible?

Team time: about 15 minutes, on paper.

  1. Team The obvious plan. Adam chooses first and takes his quickest stall. Then Ben takes his quickest of the stalls left, then Carl. Dan gets the last one. What is the total?
  2. Team Improve it by swapping. Choose two students and swap their stalls; keep the swap if the total falls. There are six pairs to try. Does any of them help?
  3. Every team How many different plans are there in all? (Adam has 4 stalls to choose from; then Ben has 3 left.) List them in order, split the list between you, and work out every total. What is the best plan, and how many students does it move from the obvious plan?
  4. Every team Go back to the obvious plan. Make the least bad of the six swaps, even though it makes the total worse. Then try all six swaps again. What happens?
  5. Extra With 10 students and 10 stalls, how many plans are there? If a computer checks a million plans a second, how long does it take to try every one? With 20 students?
My figures: open when your team has tried all five

7. Adam Games 7, Ben Food 5, Carl Books 26, Dan Plants 6: 44 minutes.

8. The six swaps give 62, 46, 70, 47, 78 and 57. Every one is worse. The obvious plan is stuck: no single swap improves it. That is a local optimum.

9. 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 plans. The best is Adam Books 11, Ben Games 6, Carl Food 6, Dan Plants 6: 29 minutes, 15 fewer. It moves three students at once: Adam to Books, Ben to Games, Carl to Food. A swap moves only two, so no swap could reach it in one step. Of the 24 plans, only these two are stuck.

10. The least bad swap is Adam and Carl: 46 minutes, two worse. From there, swapping Ben and Carl gives 29. One step backwards was the way out. This is the idea of simulated annealing, which the football paper uses to order its matches (its page 17): early on, accept a worse plan now and then; later, almost never.

11. 10 × 9 × … × 1 = 3,628,800 plans: under four seconds. With 20 students, about 2.4 × 1018 plans: about 77,000 years. Trying every plan stops working very quickly. For assignments like this one there is an exact shortcut, the Hungarian method, which the football paper uses to give each group its month (its page 18). Harder problems, like its order of matches, are why it turned to simulated annealing.

The method in one box

Write the model first: the decision variables, the objective, and every constraint, including "whole numbers".

Count the plans. If there are a few thousand or fewer, try every one: a table of IF formulas in a sheet, or a loop in Python. Then you know the answer is the best.

Too many to try? Start from a sensible plan and swap while it helps. Start again from several different plans. Accept the occasional worse swap to escape a local optimum. Then you have a good plan, and you must say that it may not be the best.

Report the best plan, the runner-up and the gap between them, and how much an input must change before the best plan changes.

3Use it: your Hall Problem

Which exit each class uses is an assignment; the order in which they leave is an ordering. Count your plans before you try them: 25 classes and 3 exits give 325, about 850 billion ways to choose the exits alone, before any order. So you cannot try them all. Use the bottleneck rule and the ordering rule on the Hall Problem page to cut the plans down, then swap to improve what is left.

The ordering rule is proved by swaps too. There the proof shows that each swap that puts a longer walk earlier never makes the plan worse, so swapping until the order is longest walk first always ends at the best order. A proof like that is what lets a paper say "best" and not "good". If your team has none, write "the best plan we found".

4Senior Team: go further

About 20 minutes more.

  1. Draw Suppose coaches could be hired in fractions. Draw the lines 61L + 49M = 930 and L = 12 on axes with L across and M up, and shade the plans with enough seats. Show that the cheapest fractional plan is at a corner of the shaded region, and find it. Round it up to whole coaches. Is that the best whole-number plan? This method, for fractional plans, is called linear programming.
  2. Sheet or Python The company also has small coaches: 28 seats, $1,900 each, as many as needed. Now there are three decision variables, and one table is not enough. Find the cheapest plan.
  3. Think In task 7, does it matter who chooses first? Try Carl first, then Ben, Adam and Dan. A rule like "everyone takes his quickest" depends on an order nobody wrote down.
  4. Python The Python below makes up an 8 × 8 table, finds the best plan by trying all 40,320, and then runs the swap method from 200 random starts. How often does swapping find the best? What does that say about a paper that ran a swap method once?
My figures: open when you have tried all four

1. A large coach is cheaper per seat ($50.8 against $53.1), so the cheapest fractional plan uses as many as it can: the corner L = 12, M = 198 ÷ 49 ≈ 4.04, costing about $47,700. Rounded up, 12 and 5 costs $50,200: not the best, which is 9 and 8. Rounding the fractional answer can miss the best whole-number plan.

2. 10 large, 6 medium and 1 small: 932 seats, $48,500. One small coach saves $200 on the best plan with two kinds.

3. Yes. Carl first takes Food, then Ben takes Games, Adam takes Books and Dan Plants: 29 minutes, the best. Other orders give 44, 46 or 47.

4. In my run, about one start in five ended at the best plan. The rest stopped at local optima. A paper that runs a swap method once has found a good plan, not the best; running it from many starts, and keeping the best result, is the least it should do.

In Python. Optional. Paste this into a notebook at Google Colab, signed in with your school account, and press Run.

import itertools, random

# 1. Coaches: try every plan
best = None
for L in range(0, 13):                 # large: 61 seats, $3,100, at most 12
    for M in range(0, 20):             # medium: 49 seats, $2,600
        for S in range(0, 34):         # small: 28 seats, $1,900
            if 61 * L + 49 * M + 28 * S >= 930:
                cost = 3100 * L + 2600 * M + 1900 * S
                if best is None or cost < best[0]:
                    best = (cost, L, M, S)
print("cheapest: $%d with %d large, %d medium, %d small" % best)

# 2. Stalls: minutes for each student (row) to set up each stall (column)
C = [[7, 24, 11, 30],
     [6, 5, 28, 29],
     [24, 6, 26, 28],
     [9, 16, 17, 6]]
total = lambda plan: sum(C[i][plan[i]] for i in range(len(plan)))
plans = list(itertools.permutations(range(4)))      # plan[i] = the stall of student i
print(len(plans), "plans; the best takes", min(map(total, plans)), "minutes")

# 3. Swap until no swap helps, from a random start, on a bigger random problem
def swap_search(C, plan):
    plan, better = list(plan), True
    while better:
        better = False
        for i, j in itertools.combinations(range(len(plan)), 2):
            new = plan[:]
            new[i], new[j] = new[j], new[i]
            if sum(C[k][new[k]] for k in range(len(C))) < sum(C[k][plan[k]] for k in range(len(C))):
                plan, better = new, True
    return sum(C[k][plan[k]] for k in range(len(C)))

random.seed(1)
n = 8
C8 = [[random.randint(5, 30) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]
exact = min(sum(C8[i][p[i]] for i in range(n)) for p in itertools.permutations(range(n)))
ends = [swap_search(C8, random.sample(range(n), n)) for _ in range(200)]
print("8 students: best %d; swapping from 200 random starts reached it %d times" % (exact, ends.count(exact)))

5Contest card

Optimisation in a competition

Use it when a task asks for the best plan, schedule, route or allocation, not just a good one.

In the sheet: a table of IF formulas with $ signs, MIN or MAX, and conditional formatting. In Python: loops over every plan, or itertools.permutations for orders and assignments.

What a judge wants to see: the decision variables, the objective and every constraint, written out; how many plans there are; how the best was found, and whether it is certainly the best; the runner-up; and how much an input must change before the answer changes.

What a judge distrusts: "optimal" from a method that cannot promise it; a fractional answer rounded with no check; and a best plan with no word on how close the next one is.
