The Hall Problem, for real

In a competition, judges read your paper and you never find out whether your plan would have worked. Here you find out. You meet the person who runs the dismissal, you test your model against a real assembly, and you pitch your plan. If the school agrees, one plan is tried.

Three things that are new
  • A client. The person who has to live with your answer. A judge asks "is the model good?" A client asks "will it work on Monday, and what will it cost me?"
  • A prediction made before the event. You write down what will happen, and hand it in sealed, before anyone measures. A prediction written afterwards is not a test.
  • The real hall. The Rubric's Evaluate row asks you to compare your model with something real. This is the most real comparison there is.

1Your client

At present the discipline head calls the dismissal, so the discipline head is your client. A client knows things your model does not: the school's rules, what went wrong in other years, which teachers must agree to a change. A client also decides whether your plan is tried.

Your paper was written for judges. From now on, everything you write must also work for a reader who is not a mathematician and has a school to run.

3Step 1: Meet the client

Before the meeting, each team writes five questions. Good questions are about the client's world, not about your model.

Weak and strong questions

Weak: "Do you like our plan?" You have not shown it yet. "Is our rate right?" The client does not know; you measure it.

Strong: "When a dismissal goes badly, what happens?" "What must never change, and why?" "How many minutes would you call a good dismissal, and what would make you notice an improvement?" "Who would have to agree to a new seating plan?" "Who will be at assemblies in March and April?"

Listen, do not pitch. Do not present your plan in this meeting; that is step 4. The Writer writes the answers down, in the client's own words where possible.

After the meeting, about 20 minutes, in your Team Paper:

  1. Team List everything the client told you that your paper did not know.
  2. Modeller For each, does your model already allow for it? If not, change an assumption, or what "best" means, and write why. It is normal for a client to change your problem. Real modellers expect it.
  3. Writer If you still disagree with the client about what matters, keep your measure of "best", but be ready to argue for it with a number in step 4.

4Step 2: Predict, then measure

Use your model on the dismissal as it is done now: you watched it on 25 Nov, and the client has told you how the call is given. Before Assembly 1, fill in the prediction slip and hand it to me, sealed, by the day before:

  • the time from the first call until the hall is empty;
  • the time from the first call until the last class is in its room;
  • a range for each: "between … and …";
  • the stage you expect to be slowest.

At the assembly, all three teams are the observers: see section 8. We open the predictions together at the next meeting. Then:

  1. Team Find the percentage error of each prediction, (model − real) ÷ real × 100%, as in Session 1. Was the real time inside your range?
  2. Data lead For each exit, plot the running total against the time and fit a line, as in Session 4. The slope is the real rate in students a second. Is it close to the rate you counted at a door?
  3. Modeller Which stage was furthest from your model? Rebuild the model with the measured rates.

A team whose prediction was 30% out and finds exactly why has learned more than a team that was close by luck.

5Step 3: A plan the school can run

A plan is only as good as someone else's ability to run it. The discipline head cannot read your model on the day. Turn your plan into a run pack of four parts, each a page or less:

  1. The seating plan. A diagram of the hall, with every class and the exit it uses.
  2. The call script. The exact words said at the microphone, in order, and when to say each. For example: "Exit 1: 2W, 2Y and 2H." Then, when 2H stands: "3W and 3Y."
  3. A note for form teachers, under 100 words: what is different, and what they do.
  4. If it goes wrong. What the person calling does if a staircase jams, or a class is not ready.

The tabletop run. Give your run pack to another team. Without your help, one of them reads the call script aloud while the others follow it on your seating plan and the hall diagram. Every place they stop or ask a question is a place your pack is unclear. Fix it.

Then update your prediction for your own plan, with the measured rates from Assembly 1 and a range.

6Step 4: The pitch

Five minutes, at most four slides, then five minutes of questions. The client may ask any member of the team.

  1. What we recommend, in one sentence.
  2. How much better, compared with the dismissal measured at Assembly 1, with a range.
  3. What it asks of the school, such as a new seating plan, a briefing for teachers or a practice, and what could go wrong.
  4. How the trial will be measured, and what result would show that you are wrong.

The client is not a mathematician: one clear chart is worth more than an equation. But every number you say must be one you can defend. The client fills in a short form for each team, then chooses one plan to try, a mix of plans, or none.

7Step 5 and Step 6: Try it, then report back

Before Assembly 2, every team predicts the chosen plan, not only the team that made it. Use your own model, and make the same predictions as in step 2, sealed. If your plan was not chosen, this is your chance to show that your model is the better one.

The client sends the run pack to form teachers before the day. On the day the client is in charge. Nobody from the Maths Team directs anyone. We observe exactly as at Assembly 1, with the same positions and the same sheets, so that the two assemblies can be compared.

Afterwards, each team adds a section to its Team Paper, "Testing the model against the real dismissal": your predictions and the measured times for both assemblies, the percentage errors, and what your model missed.

Is the new plan really better?

One assembly is one measurement. Two ordinary dismissals are never exactly the same: a speech runs late, it rains, a year group is out. So a gain only counts if it is larger than the usual difference between two ordinary dismissals.

Assembly 1 alone cannot tell us that difference. If we can, we measure one more ordinary assembly after the trial, so that there is an ordinary dismissal on each side of it. If we cannot, ask the client how much the dismissal usually varies, and write that it is the client's estimate, not a measurement.

If the gain is much larger than that difference, one trial is good evidence. If it is smaller, one trial cannot show it either way. Say so plainly. That is a result, not a failure.

The team whose plan was tried also writes one page for the client: what happened, compared with what you predicted; whether to keep the plan, change it or drop it; and what to measure next time.

8How the assemblies are measured

We need at least ten observers, so all three teams take part. I will give each of you a position and a sheet before the day. The exits are numbered as on the hall diagram.

  • If your measure of "best" needs something else counted, such as how many students are on a staircase at once, ask me by step 3. We add a position if we have the people.

The observer's rules. Count and time only. Do not record names, and do not photograph or film anyone. Stand against the wall, out of the stream. Never stop, hurry or direct anyone. If a teacher asks you to move, move. If something looks unsafe, tell the nearest teacher; do not act yourself.

9Senior Team: go further

  1. A range by simulation. In the senior study pack each class's rate was any value from 0.8 to 1.2 students a second. Replace it with the spread of the rates you measured at the three exits at Assembly 1. Give the middle 90% of 500 runs as your prediction range for Assembly 2, and state your rules so that anyone could repeat them.
  2. Is the rate steady? Look at the running totals from Assembly 1. Does the count start slowly, like a sprinter, and slow down again at the end? Fit a line to the middle part only, and say how much the slope changes.
  3. How many trials would convince you? Suppose two ordinary dismissals differ by d seconds. How large must a plan's gain be before one trial shows it? What would a second trial of each plan add? Write your answer as advice to the client.
