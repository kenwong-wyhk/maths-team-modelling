Senior study pack

In the senior division you meet teams who use epidemic models and simulations. This pack gives you one of each, built in a sheet, in about an hour. Both are small on purpose. The judges of a real competition warn that the simplest model that answers the question wins, and that a simulation is worth nothing unless its rules are stated.

1An epidemic in a spreadsheet

Flu reaches our school: 25 classes of 36, so 900 students. On day 0 one student is ill.

The model: three groups, updated once a day

S can still catch it. I is ill and can pass it on. R has recovered and cannot catch it again. N = S + I + R = 900.

  • new cases today = β × S × I ÷ N
  • recoveries today = γ × I
  • tomorrow: S − new cases, I + new cases − recoveries, R + recoveries

Take β = 0.5 and γ = 0.25: an ill student is ill for four days on average, and passes it on at a rate of half a student a day while almost everyone can still catch it.

The assumptions, which you should be able to defend or attack: every student is equally likely to meet every other; nobody catches it twice; ill students still come to school; nobody from outside brings it in again.

  1. Build Columns for the day (0 to 120), S, I, R, new cases and recoveries. Day 0 is 899, 1, 0. Check that S + I + R is 900 on every row, and say why it must be.
  2. Read On which day are most students ill, and how many? How many have been ill by day 120?
  3. Explain I rises when new cases are more than recoveries, that is when β × S ÷ N > γ. Show this means S > 450. Check in your sheet that the peak comes on the first day S falls below 450. Why does the number R0 = β ÷ γ = 2 decide whether an outbreak grows at all?
  4. Use Before day 0, vaccinate V students: move them from S to R. Find the smallest V for which the number ill never rises. How does it compare with N × (1 − 1 ÷ R0)?
  5. Test Raise β by 10%. By what percentage does the peak rise? Give the sensitivity, as in Session 6. Which input would you most want to measure well?
My figures: open only when your sheet is built

The peak is on day 29, with about 147 ill. By day 120 about 731 have been ill. On day 28 S is still above 450; on day 29 it is about 428. With 449 vaccinated, S starts at exactly 450: new cases equal recoveries, and the outbreak never grows. Raising β by 10% lifts the peak to about 181, a rise of 23%: a sensitivity of 2.3.

Go further. For the smooth version of this model, the fraction z of the school ever ill satisfies z = 1 − e−R0 z, where e ≈ 2.718. Solve it for R0 = 2 by trying values, and compare with your sheet. Then change your sheet to steps of half a day (halve both the new cases and the recoveries in each step). Which way does the answer move, and why?

A warning from a judge. In the 2023 MathWorks Math Modeling Challenge some teams used this model for the sales of e-bikes. The judge's commentary calls it a bit problematic there: it assumes that people buy an e-bike because they "catch" it from others, which is the very question the problem asked them to answer. A model that suits flu does not suit everything that spreads.

2One simulation

Back to the hall. One stream of 8 classes of 36 leaves through one exit, farthest room first. These walking times from the exit are invented: 62, 55, 47, 40, 32, 25, 12 and 5 seconds. At exactly one student a second, every class takes 36 seconds to pass the exit, and the last class is seated at 288 + 5 = 293 seconds.

But no class moves at exactly one student a second. A simulation asks: if the rate varies, what range of times should we expect?

The rules, stated so that anyone could repeat them
  1. Each class's rate is r = 0.8 + 0.4 × RAND(): any value from 0.8 to 1.2 students a second, all equally likely, each class independent of the others.
  2. A class takes 36 ÷ r seconds to pass the exit.
  3. Classes pass one after another, farthest room first. Each starts when the one before has passed.
  4. A class is seated when it has passed the exit and walked to its room.
  5. One run's result is the time the last class is seated: the largest of the eight.
  1. Build One row for each run: eight rates, eight passing times, the running total, the total plus each walk, and the largest. Copy the row down to make 500 runs.
  2. Report Give the median and the middle 90% (from the 5th to the 95th percentile), and draw a histogram. Your figures will not match mine or your teammates' exactly, because RAND() gives different numbers each time. Recalculate and watch how much they move.
  3. Explain The rate averages 1 a second, yet the median is above 293. Why? Dividing 36 by a slow rate costs more than dividing by a fast rate saves.
  4. Prove Run the same classes, with the same rates, nearest room first. Show that farthest first wins in every run, by exactly 57 seconds. A start: every pass takes at least 30 seconds.
My figures: open only when your simulation runs

From 100,000 runs: median about 297 seconds, middle 90% from about 277 to 317 seconds. The average time for a class to pass is about 36.5 seconds, not 36. (Exactly, it is 36 × ln 1.5 ÷ 0.4, for those who know logarithms.)

What the judge's commentary asks of every simulation: state each rule and each random distribution in the paper, so that a reader could repeat it without your code; and report the spread, with a histogram, a middle value and a range, not one number. The judges' notes on the "excellent" e-bike paper criticise it for exactly this.
