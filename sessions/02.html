Today's Maths Tool

Speed over a stretch. Distance ÷ the time taken for that stretch. From 10 m to 20 m he takes 2.88 − 1.89 = 0.99 s, so his speed there is 10 ÷ 0.99.

A model in stages. One speed for the whole race was last week's model. This week the race has stages, speeding up and then top speed, and each stage has its own rule. Mathematicians call this a piecewise model. Where one stage ends and the next begins is your decision, written down as an assumption.

Team time: 35 minutes. Parts 1 to 5 below are one block of work. In class they are on your team's card, and I do not interrupt.

Only the first runner starts from standing still. The other three are already moving when the baton reaches them, so the slow first 10 metres happens once, not four times.

Our first model assumed every runner starts from rest. That assumption was wrong, and now we know which one to fix.

3The handover

The rules give the next runner room to get moving. Around each 100 m line there is a zone 30 m long:

  • Start zone, 10 m (from 80 m to 90 m of the leg). The next runner may start running anywhere in here, but the baton may not change hands.
  • Handover zone, 20 m (from 90 m to 110 m). The baton must change hands inside this zone, or the team is disqualified.

So the baton can change hands as early as 90 m or as late as 110 m. The presentation version shows both.

5Test the model

  1. Team The real team was slower than your second model. List everything the model assumes that is not quite true of a real relay team. Aim for four.
  2. Writer Which one do you think costs the most time? Be ready to say why.
Open only when your team has its list
  • Not every runner is Bolt. A real team has one, at most.
  • Passing a baton takes care. Two runners at full speed must match each other, and that costs time.
  • Nobody hands over at the very edge. One step late and the team is disqualified.
  • Runners tire. Some legs are longer than 100 m, and the model keeps top speed to the end.

Each of these is an assumption you could fix next. A model is finished when it is good enough for the question, not when it is perfect.

The Maths Tool as formulas

Write T(d) for the time Bolt needs to cover d metres from the blocks. Your sheet gives T(10) = 1.89, T(20) = 2.88 and so on. The speed over one 10-metre stretch is

10 ÷ (T(d) − T(d − 10))

and the model in stages says that from 50 m on he holds his top speed of 12.17 m/s:

T(d) = 5.47 + (d − 50) ÷ 12.17   for d ≥ 50

Go further. These are not needed for the session. They are for anyone who wants more mathematics.

  1. Show that for d ≥ 50 the formula can be written T(d) = 1.36 + d ÷ 12.17. What does the 1.36 seconds mean? Check it with d = 100.
  2. Use the 1.36 seconds to explain both models in one line each. The first model, four Bolts from the blocks, pays the 1.36 seconds four times. A perfect relay would pay it once: how long would the baton take? How much time does your second model lose at its three handovers, with the early handover and with the late one?
  3. The real record, 36.84 s, is how far above the perfect relay of question 2? That gap is everything your model leaves out. Share it out among the four items on your list, with a reason for each share.
  4. Let the baton change hands at x metres, where x is between 90 and 110. Show that the second model's total time is T(x) + 2 × T(x + 20) + T(120) − 3 × T(x − 80) Check it gives 35.11 for x = 90.
  5. Use question 4 to prove that, in this model, a later handover is always faster. A start: moving the handover 1 metre later adds 3 metres at top speed, and removes 3 metres that were run at the outgoing runner's speed when he has gone x − 80 metres.

6The modelling cycle

What you have done twice now has a name: the modelling cycle. It has five steps.

StepWhat it meansToday
1 AnalyseUnderstand the real problem.Why is a relay faster than four sprints?
2 FormulateMake assumptions and build a model.Every runner is Bolt; the baton changes hands at 90 m.
3 SolveWork the model out.Your spreadsheet.
4 InterpretSay what the number means in the real world.A relay time of 35.11 s.
5 EvaluateCompare with reality and find what to fix.1.73 s too fast: what did we leave out?

Evaluating is not the end. It sends you back to step 2, to change an assumption and go round again. From now on I will use these five names.
