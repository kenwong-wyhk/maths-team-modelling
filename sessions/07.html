Session 7

From paragraphs to a paper

For six weeks you have written one section of the Hall Problem a week. Today they become one paper. First you see what judges do with a whole paper: they check that every part of the question is answered, and they read the summary before anything else.

Roles move on one place for the last time. At the end of today each team proposes the roles it will keep for the Mock Competition and the competitions after it.

1Cold start: a real problem with several parts

This is the problem from the MathWorks Math Modeling Challenge 2023, a competition for school teams in the United States and the United Kingdom: Ride Like the Wind Without Getting Winded: the growth of e-bike use. I hand each team a printed copy.

  1. Team · 5 min Read every sentence, not only the questions. List everything a judge will check that you did. Beside each, write the words in the problem that ask for it.
The list: open only when your team has made its own
  1. A model that predicts the growth of e-bike sales (Q1).
  2. Two numbers from it: the sales two years from now, and five years from now (Q1).
  3. A choice of country, the United States or the United Kingdom or both, stated.
  4. One or more factors behind the growth, and an argument, using a model, for whether each was a significant reason (Q2).
  5. The effect on carbon emissions, traffic congestion, health, or other factors you choose, as numbers (Q3).
  6. A one-page executive summary, written as a brief to the head of the transport department of the United Kingdom or the United States.
  7. At most 20 pages, not counting the code and the references.
  8. Every source cited, including the competition's data file if you use it.
  9. Any code you wrote, as an appendix. This is needed only for the computing prize.

The problem numbers three questions, but a judge checks nine things. Number 6 is the one teams forget: the summary is written to a person, who must be able to act on it. A task with no section in your paper is a task you have not answered.

  1. Team · 4 min Which would a judge rate higher? Write the first sentence of the judges' note on the weaker one.

The judge's commentary on this problem makes a point no team found: the sales figures run through the 2020 lockdowns, so a model could be split into two parts, before and after. That is the piecewise model from Session 2. The judge also warns that many teams used complicated methods where a simple model would do, and that the analysis of a model matters as much as the model itself.   3. Modeller Map the tasks. Then check that every assumption has its four parts and is used somewhere. Delete any that is not.
  4. Data lead Bring every chart and table up to the Paper Standards: a number and a title, labelled axes with units, the source, and a sentence in the text that refers to it. Then make the table of symbols, and number the references, with a citation in the text wherever each is used.

You do not need LaTeX: judges give no marks for typesetting. If your team wants it, AI may typeset your finished text, and you check the result.

Hint card: open only after ten minutes of trying

A summary in four parts, one short paragraph each:

  1. The question. What is being decided, and for whom.
  2. What we did. The model in one or two sentences, with the assumption it rests on most.
  3. What we found. The plan, the number that shows it is better than Plan A, and a range.
  4. What we recommend. The plan, and the one thing to check before trusting it.

4What you found: the summary test

  1. Team · 3 min Swap summaries with another team, and read only the summary. Write down: the plan it recommends, the number behind it, and the first place you got lost. Hand it back.

If the readers wrote a different plan or a different number from the one you meant, your summary has failed its test, however good your model is. Then one team defends its summary for three minutes; I choose who answers.

Today's habit: the judge's first five minutes

There is no new Maths Tool this week. This is what a judge does before reading closely:

  1. Read the summary. What was found, and with what numbers?
  2. Check the tasks. Is every part of the question answered?
  3. Pick one number in the summary and find it in the body. Is it the same?
  4. Look at the charts. Is each titled and labelled, with a source?
  5. Turn to the references. Are they cited in the text, or only listed at the end?

A paper that fails here is read with suspicion from then on. The Team lead does these five checks before every hand-in.

5Team Paper: a letter to the Principal

  1. Team · 5 min Start a one-page letter to the Principal: what you recommend, the number that shows it is better, what it changes for students and teachers, and how it could be tried at one assembly and timed. A frame to start from: "Dear Principal, We recommend … At present the hall takes … With our plan … We would like to try it at …, and we would measure …"

A judge reads the summary. The Principal reads the letter. Neither reads your model first.

About 45 minutes this week, in two parts, because your paper is finished this week.

  2. Team, by Wednesday evening Score the next team's paper: Junior Team A scores Junior Team B, Junior Team B scores the Senior Team, and the Senior Team scores Junior Team A. Each member takes one or two rows of the Rubric; together, go through the Paper Standards; and write down the first place you got lost. Feedback, not marks.
