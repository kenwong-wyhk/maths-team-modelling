Session 8

Mock Competition: 96 hours under IMMC rules

One problem, one paper, ninety-six hours, judged the way the International Mathematical Modeling Challenge judges papers. Its Greater China round gives a team 96 consecutive hours, and so does this mock. Nobody helps you, and that includes me.

The clock starts ten minutes into this session and stops at the same time on Monday. You hand in one paper. Two judges read it before Session 9, and in Session 9 you defend it and read what the judges wrote.

1What is different

In Session 5 you met a real competition problem cold, for forty minutes. This is the same thing at full size.

  • A real IMMC problem, from the Greater China round, with several tasks and a page limit. I hand it out on paper and as a PDF when the clock starts. The file gives the problem in English, 简体 and 繁體: read it in Chinese if that helps, and write in English.
  • No cards, no hints, no help. Everything you need is in the problem, in what you have learnt since the first meeting, and in what you can find.
  • A fixed clock. Ninety-six hours, over a weekend, as in the real round. There is no extension.
  • Real judging. Your paper gets one of the four designations of the IMMC's international round, and a cover sheet from the judges, like the ones you read on the e-bike papers in Session 7.

2The rules

These are the IMMC's rules. Where its own words are quoted, they come from its contest rules or from the note on AI printed on every 2026 problem.

  1. People Your team only. "Teams may not use any person (other than team members) to discuss or obtain ideas for solving their problem." That means me, other teachers, other teams, your family and any tutor. In the real competition a team that does is disqualified.
  2. Questions Nobody answers questions about the problem. If a sentence is unclear, decide what it most likely means, write that down as an assumption with your reason, and go on. That is what the real competition expects.
  3. Sources Any book, website, data set or software may be used, and every one is named: in the text where you use it, and in a numbered list of references at the end.
  4. AI AI may be used, openly. The IMMC asks for "honest, open and transparent disclosure", with citations in the text and details in the references, and says that using AI or not "has no impact" on the judging. Our AI Teammate Rule still holds: AI never proposes your model and never writes your paper. Keep your AI Log from the first minute. It becomes the report on AI use, in an appendix.
  5. Paper A one-page Summary Sheet, then a body of at most 20 pages, so 21 pages in all. Appendices and references come after and do not count. A4, at least 12-point type. Your team's control number and the page number at the top of every page, and no names of students or the school anywhere, as the IMMC's international rules require. One PDF, plus your spreadsheet as a separate file that an appendix describes.
  6. Hand-in At hour 96 exactly. A late paper is read, but it gets no designation. In the real competition it would not be read at all.

Two more things from the rules, because they should shape how you spend your time. "Partial solutions are acceptable," and the judges are "primarily interested in" a team's "approach and methods". And "the judges place considerable weight on the summary."

3The 96 hours

Hour 0 is when I hand out the problem, ten minutes into this session. The Team lead plans the rest, but every plan must pass these checkpoints. Each one is a photo or a link that the Team lead sends me. I read them and say nothing until Session 9.

WhenCheckpoint: what must exist
Thursday, end of this session (hour 1)The task map: every task the problem sets, the words that ask for it, and the section that will answer it. The time plan, with the hour you will freeze the model.
Friday nightAssumptions, numbered, each with its reason. A table of variables with units. A first number from a first model, however rough.
Saturday nightA rough draft of every section in your task map, however short. From here on you improve a whole paper instead of finishing half of one.
Sunday afternoon (hour 72)The model is frozen. No new model after this. The results, the sensitivity analysis and the strengths and weaknesses are written.
Sunday nightThe summary is written, last, from the finished body. The Team lead does the last check from the Paper Standards: summary and body side by side, every number ticked.
Monday (hour 96)Hand-in. Open the PDF on another device before you send it, and check every page is there.

A ten-minute check-in every day, Friday to Monday, at a time your team chooses today, in person or online. The Team lead says what is done, what is next and what is stuck. I listen. I may ask about your plan, but I will not answer anything about the problem.

Monday is a school day, so plan to finish on Sunday night. Monday is for the last look, not for writing.

4Roles

You keep the roles your team chose in Session 7. Everyone reads the whole problem in the first fifteen minutes, and anyone may be asked about any part in the defence, so nobody owns a section alone.

RoleMain job in the 96 hoursThe check only this role makes
ModellerTurns each task into mathematics; keeps the numbered list of assumptions, each with its four parts.Every assumption is used somewhere, and every task in the task map has a model.
Data leadThe team's coder. Builds the spreadsheet or code, the sensitivity table and every chart.Every number in the paper comes out of the sheet, and the sheet still gives it after the last change.
WriterWrites the paper as the work goes on, from the first evening, not at the end. Writes the summary last.Someone who was not there can follow every section.
Team leadWrites the time plan, runs the check-ins, sends the checkpoints, keeps the AI Log honest.The last check from the Paper Standards, and the hand-in.

In a team of three, the Team lead's job is combined with another role, as in Session 7.

Senior Team. The same problem. Where a tool from the senior study pack fits, use it and say why it suits the problem. Where it does not, leave it out: the judge's warning from Session 7 still holds, and the simplest model that answers the question wins.

5How your paper is judged

Before judging, I take every name off and give each paper a control number.

  1. First read · 10 min The summary, the contents and a quick look through. In a large competition this first read decides whether a paper is read in full, so your summary is your paper's first chance and maybe its only one. Each of you also does a first read of another team's paper before Session 9.
  2. Full read Two judges, me and a teacher from outside the Maths Team, read every paper in full, with the Rubric and the Paper Standards.
  3. Result A designation and a cover sheet. The cover sheet says what the judges liked and what could be better, each point with a page number. There are no marks.

The IMMC's four designations are Outstanding, Meritorious, Honorable Mention and Successful Participant. It publishes the names, not what each one takes. For this mock the judges use these descriptions:

DesignationWhat the paper does
OutstandingAnswers every task. Every assumption has a reason a sceptic would accept. The sensitivity analysis says which input the answer depends on most. The summary gives the results with their numbers, and they match the body. A judge would show it to next year's teams.
MeritoriousAnswers every task with a sound model, but has one serious gap: a task answered without a model, sensitivity missing for something the problem named, or a summary without the main number.
Honorable MentionA sound model of the main task, but at least one task is missing, or the numbers rest on choices that are never justified.
Successful ParticipantA complete paper, handed in on time, that answers part of the problem.
