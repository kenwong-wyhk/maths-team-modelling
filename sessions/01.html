Session 1

No right answer

Last week your team estimated how many basketballs would fill the InnoCourt, and three teams gave three different numbers. Today you will work on two more problems that have no answer key. Your job is to give an answer you can defend.

Today's one rule. Before you calculate anything, write down what you are assuming. No AI and no searching until the page tells you to look something up.

I will not tell you whether an answer is right. I will ask you what you assumed.

1The taxi

Problem

Amy, Betty and Ceci share one taxi home after a concert. They live along the same road.

  • Amy gets out first. The meter shows $20.
  • Betty gets out second. The meter shows $60.
  • Ceci gets out last. The meter shows $80, and that is the total fare.

How much should each of them pay?

  1. Alone · 1 min Before anyone speaks, write your own split on paper, with one reason.
  2. Team · 5 min Choose one split for the team. If you disagree, do not vote. Find out why each person thinks his way is fair.
  3. Modeller Finish this sentence for the team: "Our split is fair because we assume that …" The Writer presents it with the three amounts.

Then test your split. A split is a rule, and a judge tests a rule on cases it was not made for.

  1. Team · 3 min Test 1: alone. Alone, Amy would pay $20, Betty $60 and Ceci $80. Does your split make anyone pay more than that? Would she still want to share?
  2. Team Test 2: a fourth friend. Dora gets in too, and gets out when the meter shows $40. Split the $80 with your rule, unchanged. Does it still give an answer? Who gains most because Dora came?
What a winning team writes: open only after your team has written its own

It defines "fair" before it chooses a split, as tests that any split must pass:

  1. The three amounts add up to the fare, $80.
  2. Nobody pays more than she would have paid alone.
  3. People who ride the same stretch of road pay the same for that stretch.
  4. The rule works for any number of friends and any stops, with no new argument.

Then it checks each method against the tests. The equal split fails (b): Amy pays $26.67, and $20 alone. Sharing each stretch among those in the taxi passes all four: Amy $6.67, Betty $26.67, Ceci $46.67.

It states each assumption with its justification:

AssumptionJustification
1. The fare grows steadily with distance, so the meter reading at each stop measures the road used up to there.A taxi meter charges mainly by distance. The three live on one road, so nobody's stop adds a detour.
2. Each friend's choice, if she did not share, was a taxi of her own.The question gives no other way home. This is the price that test (b) compares with.
3. The three are equals: nobody arranged the ride or owes the others a favour.The question says nothing about who arranged it, so we treat them alike.

It says what happens if an assumption is wrong. Hong Kong taxis charge a fixed amount to start, so part of the first $20 pays for hiring the taxi at all. All three needed that, so a better split shares the fixed charge three ways and splits only the rest stretch by stretch.

Compare the two sentences. Typical: "Our split is fair because Ceci went the furthest." Winning: "We define a fair split as one where each person pays for the road she used, shared equally with everyone in the taxi on that stretch, because nobody should pay for road she did not travel."

What the tests show: open only when your team has tried both

Test 1. The equal split fails: Amy pays $26.67, and alone she pays $20.

Test 2. A rule that is only a feeling fails. "Ceci went furthest, so she pays most" cannot say what Dora pays. Sharing each stretch can: Amy $5, Dora $11.67, Betty $21.67, Ceci $41.67.

A split is a model: an assumption, turned into a rule, then tested. The taxi has no real answer to check against. The next problem does.

2The fastest human

Problem

On 16 Aug, 2009 in Berlin, Usain Bolt ran the 100 m in 9.58 seconds. It is still the world record.

Using only this number, estimate three men's world records: the 200 m, the 400 m, and the 4 × 100 m relay. For the relay, imagine four runners who are each as fast as Bolt, passing a baton.

Before any maths, write your gut guess for the 400 m record, in seconds. Keep it: you will compare it with your model.

Today's Maths Tool

Proportion as a model. time = distance ÷ speed. If the speed stays the same, twice the distance takes twice the time. That "if" is an assumption, and you chose it.

Percentage error. (model − real) ÷ real × 100%. A model says 42 s and the real time is 40 s: the error is +5%, so the model is too slow. A percentage is used because being 1 second out matters far more over 100 m than over 1500 m.

Team time: 35 minutes. Every role has a job. Roles move on one place from last week.

  1. Modeller Write every assumption before any number appears.
  2. Data lead Find Bolt's average speed in metres per second, to 2 decimal places. Then make the three estimates.
  3. Writer Bring me the three estimates. In class you get an envelope in return; here, open the box below.
  4. Team lead For each record, lead the team to a prediction: will the real record be faster or slower than your estimate? Why?

  2. For the 200 m and the 400 m you used the same method. One estimate is probably very close and the other far off. Which assumption worked for one distance and failed for the other?
  3. Is "very close" proof that your assumption was true? Think about the first few seconds of a race.
  4. The relay is faster than four world-record runs added together. Write down your team's best explanation. We start Session 2 from here.

  6. Challenge card Bolt's 10.44 m/s is too fast for the long races, and a slower speed would be too slow for the short ones. Find the one speed that makes the largest of the five percentage errors as small as possible. There is an exact answer, and a short argument for it.
The Maths Tool as formulas

Write d for the distance in metres, t for the time in seconds and v for the speed. The model "the speed stays the same" is

t = d ÷ v,   with v = 100 ÷ 9.58 = 10.44

and the percentage error of a model is

e = (model − real) ÷ real × 100%

Go further. These are not needed for the session. They are for anyone who wants more mathematics.

  1. Call the real average speed of a record u, so u = d ÷ (real time). Show that the percentage error of the model t = d ÷ v is (u ÷ v − 1) × 100%. So the error depends only on the two speeds, not on the distance.
  2. Use question 1 to prove the answer to the challenge card. The fastest record has average speed 10.44 m/s and the slowest of the five has 7.28 m/s. Show that the speed making the largest error as small as possible is exactly halfway between them, and that the largest error is then (10.44 − 7.28) ÷ (10.44 + 7.28).
  3. A better model. Try t = 9.58 × (d ÷ 100)b The model of today is b = 1. Use a calculator to find, by trying values, the b that makes the model exact for the 1500 m (206 s). Then test that b on the 200 m, 400 m and 800 m. Is its largest error smaller than 18%?
  4. Use your model from question 3 to predict the 10,000 m record. The real record is 26 minutes 11 seconds. A model fitted to races up to 1500 m is being used far outside them. What happened?

3What you just did

With the runners today you did four things, and the first two you had already done with the taxi and the basketballs:

  1. Assumed something to make the problem simple enough to work on.
  2. Calculated an answer from those assumptions.
  3. Compared the answer with the real world.
  4. Explained the gap by going back to the assumptions.

That is mathematical modelling. The calculation was the easy part.

4Paper study: how a winning team does it

Every session includes a look at a real award-winning paper, with one task. Today's paper is from the International Mathematical Modeling Challenge 2024. It answers question A from our first meeting: is this household ready to own a pet?

  1. Team · 3 min Find the pattern. What does every assumption on this page have, and in what order?
  2. Team Read the justifications for Assumption 1 and Assumption 2. One says something about how the real world behaves. The other says mainly that it makes the model simpler. Which is stronger?

Notice that the paper's own words are the team's. You are studying how they set out their thinking, not collecting sentences to reuse.

5Team Paper: this week's paragraph

Your Team Paper is your team's paper on the Hall Problem: what is the best way to dismiss the whole school from the hall? It is one shared Google Doc that grows every week, and by Session 7 it is a complete competition paper. I read all three every week, and the next session opens with the best sentence I find. Its first section is set out like this:

Assumption: A runner's average speed is the same over any distance.
Justification: It lets us use direct proportion, and it should be roughly true when the distances are similar.

  1. Team · 7 min The Writer types and the others say what to write: three assumptions you need before you can estimate how long the hall takes to empty, each with its justification. Borrow the layout above, not my words.

An assumption without a justification is a guess. An assumption with one can be tested, argued with and improved.
