The football paper, from start to finish

Two teams answered the IMMC 2025 problem very differently, and both won. This is the first of the two. Read it as a judge would, with the problem beside you and a pencil in your hand, and keep your notes: the next guide puts the second paper beside this one. About 50 minutes. The Senior Team has more at the end.

You need the 2025 problem and the paper of Team 2025006, on paper or on screen. The file has 79 pages, but you need only pages 1 to 30. From page 31 it is computer code, so do not print it. The page numbers here are the ones printed at the top of each page ("5 of 79"), which match the file. The paper's own contents page is one page out: each section starts a page earlier than it says.

Borrow how the paper is built, never its sentences. Read it yourself. If a word stops you, use the list below or a dictionary, not an AI summary: the point is what you notice.

My list: open when yours is written
  1. Choose the sport: a team sport with at least five players on the field (see the Glossary).
  2. Choose 20 teams, at least two from each continent except Antarctica, and explain the criteria.
  3. Name other things a fair schedule should consider, beyond travel, number of games and equal matchups, and discuss the trade-offs.
  4. Build a model for a fair and practical schedule: a fair share of games, equal travel, balanced matchups.
  5. Use it to make a full season schedule.
  6. Give evidence that the schedule is fair, sustainable and geographically representative.
  7. Say how many games each team must play to declare a winner.
  8. Add four teams, and explain the criteria.
  9. Say how the four affect the schedule: fairness, sustainability, geography.
  10. Say how many games are needed now.
  11. Analyse changes to key constraints: travel distances, a team's home, the number of games.
  12. In two pages or less, adapt the model to other sports.
  13. A one-page letter for a non-technical reader.
  14. A one-page visual graphic of the 20-team schedule, on its own page.
  15. Summary, contents, references, an AI report if AI was used, and no more than 24 pages before the references.

2Read with a pencil

Twelve stops, about 35 minutes. At each, read the pages, then do the task in the margin of your copy or in a note. Keep moving: if a task takes more than three minutes, write a question mark and go on. You may skip page 9, pages 11–12, page 15 and page 17, and everything after page 28.

  1. p. 1 Read only the summary. In one sentence, what does the team recommend? Then count the results the summary gives: decisions or numbers, not methods. Keep both.
  2. p. 3, 12–13 The visual graphic promises "81% fewer carbon emissions" than a double round robin. Find the two numbers behind it on page 13 and check the 81%. Is it carbon that the team measured? Find the assumption on page 12 that lets them call it carbon.
  3. p. 5–6 Before any mathematics, the team says what "fair" means. Find two meanings of fair that pull against each other. Then find, on page 16, the rule the team makes to settle it.
  4. p. 6–7, 2 Why football? List the team's reasons. The problem says some sports "already have an established international presence", and football has the World Cup. Where does the paper say why a football league is still needed?
  5. p. 7–8 The criteria for choosing teams. Beside each, find its reason and its source. Then: why are fan count and funding put on a 0-to-1 scale with z-scores, and not with min-max like the others?
  6. p. 12–13 Seven formats, each played 1,000 times by computer. IF is the team's overall score for a format: higher is better. L, the "loss", grows when the final ranking puts teams the wrong way round: lower is better. Find the winner and the runner-up by IF. How far apart are they? Which format has the lowest L? Why didn't it win?
  7. p. 14, 20 Page 14 says each team plays 9.2 games on average, from 2 × 92 ÷ 20. Check it. Then account for all 92 games. Page 14 and the start of page 20 tell you the stages. A round robin of n teams has n(n − 1) ÷ 2 games; a knockout of 8 teams has 7.
  8. p. 16 The three rules on the order of matches. One gives a number of hours for jet lag. Write it down, with its source. You will need it in the next paper.
  9. p. 18–19 Time of day. Read Assumptions 7 and 8. What is assumed about the whole world? Is a reason given? Then Figure D.2: why does the match start at 4 p.m. in Uruguay?
  10. p. 21 The four extra teams. Find the sentence where the team admits a conflict with one of the IMMC's goals. Do you accept the reason that follows it?
  11. p. 22–23 The sensitivity analysis moves each weight by 10%. Does the chosen format ever lose? In which change does it come closest to losing, and to which format? Compare section 7 of the pet paper.
  12. p. 28 The Report on Use of AI. What was AI used for? The problem's AI policy (its last pages) asks for the exact question and the complete answer. Does this report give both?
What I found: open when you have done all twelve
  1. Mine: a grouped single round robin followed by knockouts, with group games on weekends from February to June. The summary gives three results: the format, the months, and that the sensitivity test left the choice unchanged. Keep your count: the next paper's summary is built differently.
  2. 1,464 flight hours for the chosen format against 7,567 for the double round robin: 1 − 1464 ÷ 7567 ≈ 0.81. The team measured flight hours. Assumption 6 says take-off, landing and travel inside a country add little, so carbon goes with flight time. Fair enough, but the graphic does not tell the reader that it rests on an assumption.
  3. Close, balanced matches pull against a ranking that lets teams move: if teams only meet teams like themselves, nobody can climb out of their level. The settlement on page 16: no team plays more than two much stronger, or three much weaker, opponents in a row. A definition on page 5 becomes a constraint on page 16. Finding that link is what reading a whole paper is for.
  4. Its reasons: the most fans in the world, easy to play, popular in developing countries, and a good test before other sports. Section 2.1 never mentions the World Cup. The nearest answer is in the letter (page 2): other international tournaments are held in one place, while this league plays in every country, in front of home crowds. Remember this. The next paper rules football out because of the World Cup.
  5. Each criterion has a "Justification" and a source in brackets: a habit worth borrowing. A few countries with huge populations are outliers. With min-max they would score 1 and squash every other country near 0. Choosing the method to fit the data, and saying why, is Session 3 done properly.
  6. Format 5 wins with 0.721. Format 1, a full single round robin, is second with 0.704: a gap of 0.017. The double round robin (format 2) has the lowest loss, 0.81, but it needs 380 games and 7,567 flight hours, and flight hours carry the largest weight. The Senior Team will see how close 0.721 and 0.704 really are.
  7. 2 × 92 ÷ 20 = 9.2. Five groups of 4: 5 × 6 = 30. The qualifier round robin of the 10 teams placed 2nd and 3rd: 45. The placement round robin of the 5 teams placed 4th: 10. The knockout of 8: 7. Total 92. The placement round robin is mentioned only on page 20, six pages after the count, so a reader has to hunt for it.
  8. 5 hours, from Heid & Wu (2023). East-to-west trips count 1.5 times, because they take longer to recover from.
  9. That everyone in the world watches television at the same hours as people in the United States. No reason is given and nothing is tested. 4 p.m. in Uruguay is 9 p.m. in Italy. Every start from 4 to 8 p.m. was about as good, and Italy's higher fan index tipped it.
  10. "Unfortunately, all four of the new teams are European, which seems to conflict with our goals for geographical representation." The reason that follows: the index weighs many things, and strong teams such as Iran and Senegal lose out on green energy. Yours to judge. I like the honesty. "It is unlikely that the long term harm of this is prevalent" is a claim with nothing behind it.
  11. It never loses. It comes closest when the weight on flight hours (Ta) goes down: 0.725 against format 1's 0.721. Unlike the pet paper, the team shows its results and explains them: format 1 ranks the teams more accurately than format 5 but flies far more, so it gains when flying counts for less.
  12. GitHub Copilot's autocomplete while writing code, and one ChatGPT question about making boxes in Google Docs. Both are given in full: the ChatGPT answer runs on to page 30, which is why the report looks long. Keep this in mind for the next paper's report.
Beat the team's months · 5 min

Each group plays its games in its own month. The table on page 18 gives each group's average temperature in each month, and the "cost" is how far that is from 20 °C. A lower total is better.

  1. Alone Work out the total cost of the team's choice, shown under the table.
  2. Alone Try to find a cheaper choice by swapping two groups' months. There are 120 ways in all.
  3. Pair The Asian Pacific group is Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand. When it is spring in Japan, it is autumn in Australia. What can one average temperature for that group hide?
My figures: open when you have tried all three

6 + 2.75 + 1.5 + 1.25 + 0.25 = 11.75. Nothing beats it: the best alternative, swapping the Asian Pacific and South American months, costs 12.0. An average of a cold place and a warm place can be close to 20 °C while neither country is. The method is right; the input hides two hemispheres.

4Senior Team: go further

About 30 minutes more. A sheet helps.

  1. p. 16 Derive the 190: solve 1 ÷ (1 + 10(RB − RA) ÷ 400) > 0.75 for RB − RA. Check the sign of the paper's answer.
  2. p. 30 Appendix A.1. Find the weights with =GEOMEAN of each row, divided by their total. Estimate λmax: multiply the matrix by your weights (=MMULT), divide each result by its weight, and average. Then CI = (λmax − 5) ÷ 4 and CR = CI ÷ 1.12. Does the matrix pass the usual test, CR < 0.1? The pet paper checked this; this paper does not.
  3. p. 10, 13, 30 Appendix A.2 is the matrix behind the format weights. Find its weights as before, and compare them with page 10's 0.21, 0.34, 0.07, 0.10, 0.28. Then put the seven formats' Tg, Ta, σN, PA = σA ÷ Ta and L in a sheet, and min-max each column with lower as better: (max − x) ÷ (max − min). With page 10's weights you should get close to the paper's 0.721 and 0.704. Now use your weights. Which format wins?
  4. p. 21 Ecuador's nine games, with Elo ratings in brackets. Check them against the rules on page 16. Does any opponent count as much stronger or much weaker? Are there ever more than two home or two away games in a row?
  5. p. 10, 13 Each format is played 1,000 times, but no measure comes with a range. What would you need to know before believing that 0.721 beats 0.704? How would you find it?
My figures: open when you have tried all five
  1. 10(RB − RA) ÷ 400 < 1/3, so RB − RA < 400 log10(1/3) ≈ −190.8: team A must be about 190 points above B. The paper prints "= 190", which should be −190. Its conclusion on page 17 is right; the line before it has a sign slip.
  2. About 0.547, 0.140, 0.106, 0.050 and 0.157. λmax ≈ 5.96, CI ≈ 0.24, CR ≈ 0.21: more than twice the limit. The weights on page 8 (2.84, 0.78, 0.62, 0.33, 1) are the exact eigenvector, scaled so the last is 1; scaling does not change the ranking. The formula on page 8 then prints two of them as 0.63 and 0.333.
  3. About 0.20, 0.32, 0.07, 0.09, 0.32: the matrix gives the last measure, L, more weight than page 10 does. With page 10's weights I get 0.724 for format 5 and 0.706 for format 1, close to the paper's figures. With the matrix's own weights the two formats tie at about 0.717. With the exact eigenvector, format 1 edges ahead, 0.720 to 0.715. Either the matrix or the printed weights has a slip, and the choice of format turns on it. The team half-says so on page 23: format 1 is "the closest contender".
  4. Ecuador is 1911. The biggest gap is to France, 2031: 120 points, well short of 190. So the skill rule never applies to Ecuador. The pattern is home, away, away, home, home, away, away, home, home: never more than two in a row, five at home and four away.
  5. How much each average would move if the 1,000 seasons were played again with new random numbers: a standard deviation, or simply the whole simulation run twice. Without it, a gap of 0.017 might be chance. A paper that plays 1,000 seasons can afford to show this.
