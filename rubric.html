The Rubric

There are no marks in the Maths Team. This page replaces them. It has one row for each step of the modelling cycle and one for communication, and it says what a piece of work looks like at three stages.

I use it when I read your Team Paper. You use it when you read another team's work, and your own. A judge in a competition is asking the same questions: the international challenge gives no numerical scores, accepts partial solutions, and says its judges are mainly interested in a team's approach and methods.

RowStartingDevelopingStrong
Analyse
Understand the real problem.		The problem is copied out. Words such as "best" are left as they are.The problem is restated in the team's own words, and it says what will be measured.The restatement says what is being decided, for whom, and what is left out and why. Every part of the question is listed, and the paper answers each one.
Formulate
Make assumptions and build a model.		Assumptions are missing, or appear after the numbers.Assumptions are written first, each with a reason. Variables are named.Each reason would convince someone who disagrees, and each assumption says where it is used and what would happen if it were wrong. The team says why this kind of model suits the problem, and which assumption matters most.
Solve
Work the model out.		There is an answer, but the working cannot be followed.The working can be followed and repeated by another team.The working is checked a second way, and a sensible tool was chosen for it.
Interpret
Say what the answer means.		A number is given with no units or meaning.The result is stated plainly in the body of the paper, in words, with units, as an answer to the question.The answer says how sure the team is, and what someone with no mathematics should do because of it.
Evaluate
Compare with reality and find what to fix.		The model is not tested.The team says how the model could be tested, compares it with something real, and lists its strengths and weaknesses.The team changes one input at a time to see what moves the answer, and says what it would fix next.
Communicate
Make a reader believe you.		Only the team could follow it.A reader who was not there can follow it from start to finish. It is short and well ordered, every chart has a title, labelled axes with units and a source, and every source of data or ideas is named.A one-page summary in the team's own words gives the problem, the approach and the conclusion, so that a reader wants to read on, and every number in it matches the body. Long working is moved to an appendix. Any use of AI is reported honestly.

1Where it comes from

The rows follow the guidelines that the International Mathematical Modeling Challenge gives to teams: restate the problem; set out all variables and assumptions; justify the model you use; say how it could be tested; discuss its strengths and weaknesses; state results plainly; be concise and organised; write a one-page summary; credit every source; and report any use of AI. The Hong Kong competition has not published its judging criteria. Its final includes a presentation, and the same rows apply when you present aloud.

The plain things a judge checks before reading closely are on a separate page, the Paper Standards.

One thing comes before every row: the work must be your team's own. A team that gets help with the problem from any person outside the team is disqualified. Books, websites, data and AI tools may be used, as long as every one is named.

2How to give feedback with it

  • Choose one or two rows, not all six.
  • Write one thing the team did well, and point to the sentence that shows it.
  • Write one question you would ask them as a judge. A question is more useful than a judgement.
  • Do not give a mark or a level. The columns are there to show what the next step looks like.
