The pet paper, from start to finish

You have met this paper in three Paper Studies, one page at a time. Now read the whole of it, as a judge would: with the problem beside you, a pencil in your hand, and one question in your head. Is this enough? About 50 minutes. The Senior Team has more at the end.

You need the problem and the paper I handed out in Session 3, on paper or on screen. The page numbers here are the ones printed at the top of each page of the paper; its page 1 is the third page of the file.

Borrow how the paper is built, never its sentences. Read it yourself. If a word stops you, use the list below or a dictionary, not an AI summary: the point is what you notice.

1Before you read: what was asked?

  1. Alone · 5 min Read the three numbered questions of the problem, and the list under "Your PDF submission should consist of". Skip the rules after it. Write a list of every task, including the small ones inside brackets. Aim for at least ten. You will use the list at the end.
My list: open when yours is written
  1. Define "pet".
  2. Build a model that decides whether a household is ready for a cat.
  3. Use no more than ten inputs from a household.
  4. Draw a diagram of how the model decides.

2Read with a pencil

Twelve stops, about 30 minutes. At each, read the pages, then do the task in the margin of your copy or in a note. Keep moving: if a task takes more than three minutes, write a question mark and go on. You may skip pages 7–9 (the matrix), pages 15–17 (the other pets) and everything after page 21.

  1. Abstract Read only the abstract, on the first page of the file. In one sentence, what does the team say it did? Keep the sentence. You will check it at the end.
  2. p. 1–2 The team restates the problem as a list of tasks. Put it beside your list. Which task from the problem is missing from theirs? Watch whether the paper ever does it.
  3. p. 2 The five assumptions, from Session 1. For Assumption 5, find a later page where it is used.
  4. p. 3–5 Count the inputs a household must give. Does the model keep to the problem's limit?
  5. p. 6 Equation 4.1 scores space. Sketch its graph: score up the side, space along the bottom. Mark Smin and Sopt. Now put numbers on the space axis. Does the paper give you any?
  6. p. 10–11 An allergy makes the unsuitability score infinite. Why infinite, and not just a large weight? Then follow one household through the flowchart on page 11. Could someone who has not read the paper do the same?
  7. p. 12–13 The six families. Find the word "presumably" on page 12. What does it tell you about the money figures? Then find a family whose description contradicts itself. Which family is closest to the 0.6 line?
  8. p. 14 Shanghai. Check equation 4.14 with a calculator. The formula multiplies the proportions, so it treats them as independent. Name two of them that probably go together.
  9. p. 18 Equation 6.2 gives next year's number of households, with a ceiling K. Where in the text is the value of K given?
  10. p. 19–20 Section 7 explains a sensitivity test. Find its result.
  11. p. 20 Strengths and weaknesses. Which strength has nothing in the paper to back it?
  12. p. 21 The letter. Could a director who has never seen the paper act on it? Underline the sentence you think a director would act on.
What I found: open when you have done all twelve
  1. Yours to keep.
  2. The one that matters: their list leaves out "by species" from question 3, and the paper never does it. The predictions on page 19 are for all pets together. (Their list also leaves out defining "pet", which they do anyway on page 3, and naming the region of the example households, which they never do.)
  3. The 0.6 threshold is used in the decision rules on page 11 and to sort the families on pages 12–13.
  4. Eight: five for the readiness score and three deal-breakers. The limit is ten.
  5. No. Smin and Sopt are never given, for space or for anything else. The families' scores were typed straight into the team's code (page 24), so the formula is never used with numbers.
  6. It is a veto. No amount of space or money makes an allergy safe, so no score is allowed to make up for it. A weight, however large, can always be outweighed.
  7. The team is not sure whether the money is per day or per month: it does not know its own unit. Family 6 is said to have "the most space", at 40 square metres, the least of the six. Family 2 scores 0.607, just above 0.6.
  8. It is 9.8%. Space and money, for example: richer households tend to have bigger homes. If two proportions go together, more households have both than the product says, so the estimate comes out too low.
  9. Nowhere. It is only in the code, on page 26: 30 million households for Shanghai, more than Shanghai's whole population of 24.2 million (page 14).
  10. There is none. The section describes the test and stops: no table, no number, no conclusion.
  11. "Inclusion of Sensitivity Analysis". See stop 10.
  12. Yours. Compare with a teammate.
Run the test the team did not show · 5 min

Family 2's five scores, from the team's code on page 24: space 0.8, time 0.7, money 0.9, age 1, experience 0. The weights, from page 9: 0.125, 0.103, 0.426, 0.051, 0.295.

  1. Alone Check that Family 2's readiness score comes to about 0.607.
  2. Alone Make the money weight 20% smaller, as section 7 says. Work out the new score. Does Family 2 still pass?
My figures: open when you have both numbers

0.8 × 0.125 + 0.7 × 0.103 + 0.9 × 0.426 + 1 × 0.051 + 0 × 0.295 = 0.607. With the money weight at 0.341, the score is 0.530, and Family 2 fails. (If you noticed that the weights no longer add up to 1 and rescaled them, you get 0.579: still a fail.) The one result the team did not show is the one that changes an answer.

4Senior Team: go further

About 30 minutes more. A sheet helps.

  1. p. 8–9 Put the 5 × 5 matrix in a sheet. For each row, take the geometric mean of its five numbers (=GEOMEAN), then divide each mean by the total of all five. How close are your weights to the team's?
  2. p. 9 The team gives the largest eigenvalue as 5.408, and the formula CI = (λmax − n) ÷ (n − 1). Work out CI from their own numbers, and compare it with the CI they print.
  3. p. 8, 22 The comparisons are said to draw on reference [1]. Look it up. Does it support "money is more important than space"?
  4. p. 15 Check equation 4.17 for Shenzhen. Is 12.59 million a number of households or of people?
  5. p. 24 Find where the families' scores enter the code. What does that tell you about equations 4.1 to 4.4?
  6. p. 26–27 Find the growth rate r, the ceilings K and the retention rate in the code. Which part of question 3 does the retention rate settle?
  7. Sheet Run all ten changes of section 7 for Family 2: each weight 20% up and 20% down, one at a time. In how many does the verdict change? Then do it again with the weights rescaled to add up to 1 after each change. Which way would you defend to a judge?
My figures: open when you have tried all seven
  1. About 0.121, 0.106, 0.441, 0.050 and 0.282. The team's method gives 0.125, 0.103, 0.426, 0.051 and 0.295. Close, and money comes first either way.
  2. (5.408 − 5) ÷ 4 = 0.102. The paper prints 0.147. Its consistency ratio, 0.09, matches 0.102, so 0.147 is a slip.
  3. No. Reference [1] is an encyclopedia article on the history of domestication. The comparisons are the team's own judgement, which page 20 admits.
  4. The text calls it households, then divides by 3 as if it were people. 12,590,000 ÷ 3 × 8.7% is about 365,000; the paper prints 594,000. The Shenzhen graph on page 19 starts near 365,000, so the code and the text disagree.
  5. They are typed in by hand. The scoring formulas are never used with numbers.
  6. r = 0.01; K = 30 million for Shanghai and 20 million for Shenzhen and New York; retention is 1. So retention, which question 3 asks for, is not modelled at all.
  7. Without rescaling, cutting any weight lowers every score, and Family 2 fails in four of the ten changes (every cut except experience, where its score is 0). With rescaling it fails in two: money down (0.579) and experience up (0.573). Rescaling is easier to defend, because the weights should still add up to 1.
