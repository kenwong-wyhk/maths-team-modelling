Team Paper · Phase One

The Hall Problem

Every team writes one paper on this question, from the first meeting to Session 7. Each week you learn one tool on a small problem, then use it here.

Problem

After an assembly, the whole school leaves the hall and every class returns to its own classroom.

What is the best way to dismiss the school from the hall?

There is no answer key. You decide what "best" means, you build the model, and you defend it. If a team's plan is convincing, I will ask the school to try it: see The Hall Problem, for real.

1What you are given

  • The hall is in the Wu Jieh Yee Building, on the second floor.
  • It has three main exits. One leads down to the ground floor, past the chapel, and arrives near room C04. One leads to the first floor, near room C11. One stays on the second floor and leads towards room C21.
  • There are 25 classes, with about 36 students in each.
  • Every class goes back to its own classroom. The classrooms are on three floors of the Classroom Wing, shown below. Staircase A joins the three floors.

2What you may change

At present the discipline head calls the dismissal. Your paper may propose something different. You are free to design:

  • The seating plan: where each class sits in the hall.
  • The exits: which exit each class uses.
  • The order: who leaves first, who waits, and how the call is given.

You may not change the building, the classrooms or the number of students.

3What you must find out yourselves

Nobody will give you these. A model is only as good as the numbers in it, so measure what you can and write down the rest as assumptions.

  • How wide each exit is, and how many students pass through it in a minute.
  • How long it takes to walk each route, including the stairs.
  • Where the crowd slows down or stops, and why.
  • How long a dismissal takes now.

How to collect data. Count and time only. Do not record names, do not photograph or film anyone, and do not hold anyone up. Ask me before you go into the hall to measure.

4Two tools for the decision

Comparing two plans tells you which is better. These two tools help you find the best one. Use them after Session 6.

The bottleneck rule

A stream of students passes through several stages in a row: out of the seats, through an exit, down the stairs, along a corridor. However fast the other stages are, the stream can go no faster than its slowest stage. If the slowest stage lets r students through each second, and N students must pass it, then the time for the stream is about

T ≈ N ÷ r + w

where w is the time the last student then takes to walk to his room. So find the slowest stage first: speeding up any other stage changes almost nothing.

The ordering rule

Several classes leave one after another through the same exit. Class i takes pi seconds to pass the exit, then walks for wi seconds to its room. If you want the last class to be seated as early as possible, send the classes in order of longest walk first.

Why: take any two classes next to each other in the queue, where the one with the shorter walk goes first. Swap them. The class now going first finishes its walk no later than the second class did before, and the class now going second finishes at the same moment as the pair did before, but has a shorter walk. So the swap never makes the last arrival later. Keep swapping until the order is longest walk first.

Go further.

  1. Write the proof above with symbols. Start the pair at time S, with walks wi < wj, and compare the larger of the two finishing times before and after the swap.
  2. If every class has the same size, show that the average time a class is seated is the same for every order. So "best" must mean the last class, not the average class, for the order to matter. Which does your paper use?
  3. When might "longest walk first" be a bad plan in the real building? What would your model need to include to see it?
